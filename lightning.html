Does art have a religious structure? When I think about works like The Lightning Field (1977) I think about historical specificity, about site and place in the way that earthworks and these long-maintained pieces have been theoretically prescribed via art history. The institutionalization of a gesture, or the monumentalization of poetry. There is something powerful about this, about the inaccessibility of the piece now, perhaps more than in the 1970s, because of the demands for legibility, visibility and self-branding on working artists today. The idea that visitors can interact with this piece or others like it in a purely unmediated way is ultimately a fantasy, but the constraints and difficulty are meaningful; the desire for art to deeply affect someone in such a way that it shocks them out of their stupor is a noble one. Especially so when external factors in travel, weather, distance etc. become a condition of experiencing the work. Perhaps Lighting Field is for the seekers. Adorno: “It has become self-evident that nothing concerning art is self-evident anymore, not its inner life, not its relation to the whole, not even its right to exist.” When I go to new cities, I often seek out museums, project spaces, instantiations of culture that are specific to the locale. Culture comes as something particular to a place, in an inextricable way, or as a type of feeling. Modern and contemporary art, the kind that trafficks in ambiguity, experience, and ambivalence, requires a sense of belief or commitment— to be invested in it is to be committed to the foibles of an anomaly in the history of aesthetics, an aberration both fleeting and world-historical. It is in this sense devotional, for the keepers of the flame. The circuits of travel and communication suggest a sense of pilgrimage. A certain sectarian spirit seems adjacent. And of course it’s back to the enlightenment and romanticism— the power of transcendental experience, the role of rationalism and political forms versus the irrationality of so much human and folklorc culture, the sublimation of all these questions into aesthetic forms. Museums as we know have their origins in the desire to recreate religious structures for the nation-state— spectacular displays of power. It’s where stuff goes to literally be put on pedestals! Like temples for dynastic rulers, places that expressed a nolesse obilge towards the lower classes they opened their doors to under specific and ritual conditions. Perhaps De Maria’s piece is something like a counterpoint to this. Or, if you think deeply about patronage and feel contemptuous about the De Menil involvement as a kind of landlorist and power-loaded gesture, maybe it is like a distorted image, a reflection of a tendency transformed as it passes through time and circumstance. Or perhaps the piece is equally indebted to relations of power and this is another aspect to how it functions as devotional. The monumentalization of poetry might be good one one day and bad on another. It is in this light that I can think about Lightning Field (1977)
