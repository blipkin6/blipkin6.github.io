Fragmentary Notes on Conspiracy

I.

“Seeing is forgetting the name of the thing one sees.”

So said poet Paul Valéry, in a musing that became widely popularized after it was used as the title of Californian artist Robert Irwin’s 1966 retrospective. Associated with the Light and Space movement, Irwin focused on installations concerning perception, site specificity, ecology, and the exchange of spatial energies. Four years later, at age 42, Irwin ceased to have a studio and sold off all of his supplies.

“All that was once solid melts into air,” once wrote Karl Marx, describing the principle of abstraction and alienation inherent in the rise of capitalism: the transformation of labor and value, just as much as objecthood. Philosopher Marshall Berman would extrapolate this quote, found in an 1888 translation of The Communist Manifesto, to explain the contradictions between the economic engine of modernization and the presence of modernism as an artistic and cultural tendency.

By the early 1970s, the modernist obsession with a specific type of formal materialism was losing ground as the dominant artistic and philosophical viewpoint. This obsession was defined by theorists such as Clement Greenberg and Michael Fried, who railed against theatricality and artworks that lacked a sense of imminence between meaning and form.

New York, a center of both the artworld and multinational finance, is forever altered by the encounters between modernism and urban life. In a 2010 afterword to his book All That is Sold Melts into Air, Berman would recall a man walking up to him on Broadway to tell him that in designing the new identity and signage for the NYC subway that “all the time he was putting his map together, he had tried to keep my book in mind.”

II.

This is an essay about Contemporary Art and the conspiratorial.

The shared affinity between the conspiratorial and contemporary art is a relation to the production, distortion, projection, and reading of meaning. When I say ‘the conspiratorial,’ I am describing a sensibility with a relation to conspiracy theory, but which is equally influenced by spirituality, political critique, new age aesthetics, and digital culture. Conspiracy and conspiracy theory (not to mention social atomization and political extremism) are the substrate upon which culture and media act to create the conspiratorial.

Conspiracy and art both exist in a place torn between rationality and emotion, fiction and documentary, virality and impenetrability. Each is a field inexorably tied to history, to specific cultural references and forms. Conspiracy theory, like other forms of theory, is predominantly descriptive and not prescriptive. A recent rise in the amount of artwork being produced about state secrets, diagrammatic aesthetics, techno-spirituality, and ritual experiences suggests that the equally non-prescriptive field of Contemporary Art is taking notes on how much more operative

There is a difference between the theorization of conspiracy, and the form of empirically verifiable political conspiracies. Art has grown to incorporate conspiracy-as-method: a thinking through of creatively paranoid responses.

III.

To someone who exists as an outsider to the art world, broadly defined, Contemporary Art appears as if not a conspiracy, then some kind of opaque ritual.
